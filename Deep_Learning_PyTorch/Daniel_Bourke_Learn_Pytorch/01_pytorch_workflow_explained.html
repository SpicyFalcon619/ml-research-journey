0 Start here — what is a "model," actually?

Before touching PyTorch at all: forget the word "model" for a second. You already know exactly what one is.

Remember the equation of a line from school?

y  =  m×x  +  b

m is the slope, b is where the line crosses the y-axis. Pick any m and b, and you get a specific line. That's it — that's what "a model" means in this notebook. Your model is just this exact same formula, with different letters:

y_pred  =  weights×x  +  bias

Two numbers you're allowed to adjust: weights (the slope) and bias (the y-intercept). That's the entire "model" — not some mysterious AI black box, just a line with two knobs on it.

So what does "training" mean? You start with the two knobs set to random, wrong values. Then you look at real data (points that do sit on the "correct" line) and nudge the knobs, over and over, until your line's predictions land as close as possible to those real points. That's the whole idea. Every term you're about to meet — loss, gradient, backpropagation, optimizer — is just a piece of machinery for doing that nudging automatically, in code, instead of you eyeballing a graph and adjusting sliders by hand.

i
Where tensors fit into this

A tensor is just PyTorch's word for "a grid of numbers" (you already know this part). A model is the first time you use tensors for something more than storing data — here, two very small tensors (weights and bias, each holding a single number) are being treated as knobs that PyTorch is allowed to adjust by itself. Everything below is about how PyTorch decides which direction to turn each knob, and by how much.

1 The big picture, before any code

One mental model to hold onto for everything below: a model is just a guessing machine with dials on it. Training is the process of turning those dials so the guesses get less wrong.

Forward pass (blue) — data flows through the model to produce a guess, then the guess is compared to reality to get a loss number. Backward pass (amber) — that loss number flows back through the same path to figure out which dial (weight? bias?) is most to blame, and by how much. The optimizer then nudges each dial a little in the direction that reduces the loss. Repeat hundreds of times.

i
Why two passes?

The forward pass answers "what does the model currently think?" The backward pass answers "if I nudge weight up a hair, does the loss get better or worse — and by how much? Same question for bias." Gradient descent is just: nudge each dial in whichever direction the backward pass says helps, by an amount scaled by the learning rate.

2 Gradient descent, from scratch

Forget PyTorch for a minute. This is a general idea that shows up everywhere in machine learning, not just here.

The analogy: you're standing on a hillside in thick fog. You can't see the bottom of the valley, but you can feel which way the ground slopes under your feet. So you take a small step downhill. Then you feel the slope again, and take another small step. Repeat enough times and you end up at the bottom — without ever having seen the whole hill.

The "hill" here is the loss — a single number that says how wrong your model currently is. The "slope you feel" is the gradient — the direction and steepness of the loss with respect to one of the model's numbers (weight, or bias). The "step" is:

new_value  =  old_value  −  learning_rate  ×  gradient

That's the entire algorithm. Everything else — PyTorch, neural networks, GPT — is this one line, applied millions of times to millions of numbers.

What the "hill" actually looks like for your model

Your model only has two dials: weights and bias. To make this drawable, freeze bias at its true value (0.3) and only look at what happens to the loss as weights changes. That gives you an actual, real curve — computed from your 40 training points:

The loss for your model as weights varies, with bias pinned at the true 0.3. The bottom of the V sits exactly at weights = 0.7 — the true value used to generate your data. Notice it's a sharp V, not a smooth curve — that's a direct consequence of the loss function your notebook uses (nn.L1Loss), which we'll come back to.

The learning rate is the size of your step

Take that same V-shaped hill and roll a ball down it three times, changing only the learning rate. Same starting point each time — your model's actual random initial weight, 0.3367:

Left (lr = 0.002): tiny, cautious steps — safe, but would take hundreds of epochs to arrive. Middle (lr = 0.01 — your notebook's actual value): steady progress, still climbing after 30 steps. Right (lr = 0.08): steps so big it jumps clean over the target and lands on the other side, then jumps back over again next step, forever. It never crashes or diverges to infinity — it just orbits the target, overshooting every single time.

i
The learning rate is a trade-off, not a "bigger is worse" dial

Too small and training is needlessly slow. Too big and you overshoot the minimum every step — which, depending on the shape of the hill, can mean gentle oscillation (harmless) or wild divergence (fatal). lr = 0.01 in your notebook is a completely reasonable, conservative default — this is exactly why courses tell you to start around there for a simple problem like this one.

Both dials at once: the real picture

Your model actually tunes two numbers together, so the real "hill" is a 3D surface over (weights, bias) — here shown from above as a contour map, darker = lower loss. The path is the exact sequence of steps your optimizer actually takes, computed with your real learning rate (0.01), starting from your model's real random initial values.

start is the random initial guess PyTorch drew for you (weights=0.3367, bias=0.3904). The path spirals down the valley toward weights=0.7, bias=0.3 — the exact values used to generate your data — then, once it gets close, starts circling tightly around the target instead of settling exactly on it. That's the same overshooting behavior from the panel above, just in two dimensions at once.

3 Backpropagation: how the gradient actually gets computed

Gradient descent tells you what to do once you have a gradient. Backpropagation is the algorithm that computes that gradient in the first place — and it's just the chain rule from calculus, applied mechanically.

Your model's forward pass is a short chain of simple operations:

Top row, blue — the forward pass: data flows left to right through each operation, ending in a single loss number. Bottom row, amber — the backward pass: starting from the loss, each operation is asked "given how much you contributed to the error, how much of that blame belongs to your inputs?" — and that question gets passed backward, operation by operation, all the way to weights and bias.

A single data point, by hand

Full batches are tedious to trace by hand, so let's do exactly one training point and watch every number. Take the point where x = 0.1 (so the true answer is y = 0.7×0.1 + 0.3 = 0.37), using your model's real starting dials weights = 0.3367, bias = 0.3904.

If y_pred goes up a hair, does loss go up or down? d(loss)/d(y_pred) = sign(error)+1
If weights goes up a hair, how much does y_pred move? d(y_pred)/d(weights) = x0.1
If bias goes up a hair, how much does y_pred move? d(y_pred)/d(bias) = 11

That's it — that's backpropagation for this point. Multiplying local derivatives together along the path from loss back to a parameter is the chain rule, and doing it mechanically, operation by operation, is all loss.backward() does.

i
One point can't tell the whole story

Notice the gradient above says "increase weights, increase bias" — pushing bias up even though it's already slightly too high (0.3904 vs the true 0.3). That's not a mistake: with only one data point, gradient descent can't distinguish "weights is too low" from "bias is too low" — it just finds some direction that reduces error on this point. This is exactly why real training uses the average gradient over the whole batch (all 40 training points, each with a different x) — averaging over many points with different x values is what lets the optimizer tell the two dials apart and converge on the right combination of both.

Where PyTorch actually stores this

# 3. zero the gradients from the LAST iteration
optimizer.zero_grad()

# 4. backprop: walks the graph backward, computes d(loss)/d(param)
#    for every param with requires_grad=True, and STORES it in param.grad
loss.backward()  ← this is everything in the table above, done automatically

# at this point: model.weights.grad and model.bias.grad now hold real numbers

Every nn.Parameter (your weights and bias) has a hidden .grad attribute. Before backward(), it's empty. After, it holds exactly the numbers from the "chained" row of the table above — averaged across however many data points you passed through the model. requires_grad=True is the flag that tells PyTorch "track every operation this number goes through, so I can differentiate through it later."

Why zero_grad() has to come first

PyTorch accumulates (adds) new gradients onto whatever is already sitting in .grad, rather than replacing it. This is useful in advanced setups (splitting a huge batch into pieces), but for a normal loop it means: if you forget zero_grad(), epoch 2's gradient gets added on top of epoch 1's leftover gradient, epoch 3 adds a third copy on top of that, and your updates silently blow up. That's why it's step 3, before step 4 (backward) every single time through the loop.

4 The 5-step loop, tied to your exact code

This is the block your notebook repeats epochs times. Every training loop you will ever write in PyTorch — for a two-parameter toy model or a billion-parameter transformer — is a variation on these same five lines.

1
Forward pass
Run the data through the model to get a prediction.
y_pred = model(X_train)
2
Calculate loss
Compare the prediction to the real answer — one number, "how wrong."
loss = loss_fn(y_pred, y_train)
3
Zero the gradients
Clear out last iteration's leftover .grad values.
zero_grad()
4
Backward pass
Chain rule, walked backward — fills in fresh .grad values.
backward()
5
Optimizer step
Gradient descent update, applied to every parameter at once.
step()

Steps 1–2 are the forward pass from Part 1. Steps 3–5 are the backward pass + descent from Parts 2–3. Then the loop repeats from step 1 with the newly-updated weights and bias.

i
What optimizer.step() is actually doing

Your notebook sets up optimizer = torch.optim.SGD(params=model.parameters(), lr=0.01). When you call optimizer.step(), for every parameter it was given, it runs exactly the update rule from Part 2:

param.data −= lr × param.grad

The other two switches: .train(), .eval(), inference_mode()

CallWhat it's for
model.train()Puts layers that behave differently during training (dropout, batch norm — not used in this simple model, but you'll meet them soon) into "training" mode. Doesn't touch gradients by itself.
model.eval()The opposite switch, for those same layers, used whenever you're checking performance instead of training.
with torch.inference_mode():Tells PyTorch "don't bother tracking operations for backprop in this block" — since you're only predicting, not training, this saves memory and time. Always wrap test-time predictions in this.

4.5 Wait — what's a class? A 5-minute Python refresher

You're about to see class LinearRegressionModel(nn.Module): in real code. If you've never written a Python class before, that line is genuinely unfamiliar syntax — not something you forgot, something new. Here's the whole idea, with no PyTorch in it yet.

Everything you've written in Python so far has probably been: variables holding data, and functions that take some input and return some output. A class is a third thing: a blueprint for creating "objects" that bundle data and the functions that act on that data together, into one package.

A tiny, non-PyTorch example:

class Dog:                        # the blueprint
    def __init__(self, name):     # runs automatically when you MAKE a dog
        self.name = name          # this dog remembers its own name

    def bark(self):               # something every dog can DO
        print(f"{self.name} says woof!")

my_dog = Dog("Rex")   # actually create one real dog from the blueprint
my_dog.bark()         # -> "Rex says woof!"
PieceWhat it means
class Dog:Declares a new blueprint named Dog. No actual dog exists yet — this just defines what a dog would look like.
def __init__(self, name):The setup method. Python runs this automatically, once, the moment you write Dog("Rex") — it's where a brand-new object gets its starting data.
selfMeans "this particular object, right here." Every dog you create gets its own self — Dog("Rex") and Dog("Biscuit") are two separate objects, each with their own self.name.
self.name = name"This dog remembers its own name from now on." Anything stored on self sticks around for the object's whole lifetime, not just while __init__ is running.
my_dog = Dog("Rex")Actually creates one real object from the blueprint. This is the only line that produces an actual dog.
my_dog.bark()Calls the bark action on that specific dog — inside bark, self automatically refers to my_dog.

Now the PyTorch version — same idea, different data

Your model needs to (a) remember two numbers of its own (weights, bias — like the dog remembering its name), and (b) know how to do one thing (turn an x into a y_pred — like the dog knowing how to bark). That's exactly what a class is for, so that's exactly what PyTorch uses.

class LinearRegressionModel(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self):
        super().__init__()
        self.weights = nn.Parameter(torch.randn(1, requires_grad=True, dtype=torch.float))
        self.bias = nn.Parameter(torch.rand(1, requires_grad=True, dtype=torch.float))

    def forward(self, x):
        return self.weights * x + self.bias
PieceIn terms of the Dog example
class LinearRegressionModel(nn.Module):A new blueprint, but written to start from an existing blueprint called nn.Module — the (nn.Module) part means "reuse everything nn.Module already knows how to do, and add my own stuff on top." This is called inheritance. PyTorch's nn.Module is a blueprint that already knows a lot of useful bookkeeping for "being a trainable model" — this line borrows all of it for free.
def __init__(self):Same setup method as Dog — runs once, when you write LinearRegressionModel().
super().__init__()Because this blueprint is built from nn.Module, this line means "before I set up my own stuff, run nn.Module's own setup first." Skipping this line would silently break PyTorch's internal bookkeeping — always the first line inside __init__ when inheriting from something.
self.weights = ... / self.bias = ...Exactly like self.name = name — "this model remembers these two numbers as its own."
def forward(self, x):This model's version of bark() — the one action it knows how to perform, using the numbers it remembers about itself (self.weights, self.bias).

And later in the notebook:

model_0 = LinearRegressionModel()   # same as my_dog = Dog("Rex") — makes one real model
y_pred = model_0(X_train)           # same idea as my_dog.bark() — runs forward() for you

One shortcut worth knowing: writing model_0(X_train) is PyTorch's way of calling forward() for you automatically — you almost never call model_0.forward(X_train) directly, even though that's technically what happens underneath.

i
Why does any of this need to be a class at all?

Because the two numbers (weights, bias) have to persist between calls — the model needs to still remember its (possibly already-partly-trained) numbers the next time you call it, not start fresh every time. A plain function can't remember anything between calls; an object built from a class can, on self. That's the entire reason this pattern exists — it's not PyTorch being needlessly fancy, it's the minimum structure needed for "a thing that remembers numbers and updates them over time."

2. The model

This is the exact class from the Python-classes refresher in Part 4.5, in its real, complete form:

class LinearRegressionModel(nn.Module):  # almost everything in PyTorch inherits from nn.Module
    def __init__(self):
        super().__init__()
        self.weights = nn.Parameter(torch.randn(1, requires_grad=True, dtype=torch.float))
        self.bias = nn.Parameter(torch.rand(1, requires_grad=True, dtype=torch.float))

    def forward(self, x: torch.Tensor) -> torch.Tensor:
        return self.weights * x + self.bias   # same formula as the data, but with GUESSED numbers
PieceWhat it means
nn.ModuleThe base class for anything that can hold learnable numbers and define a computation. Subclassing it and writing forward() is the standard shape of every PyTorch model, from this to GPT-4-scale networks.
super().__init__()Must be the first line — it runs nn.Module's own setup so the class can track parameters, move to GPU, save/load, etc. Skipping it silently breaks all of that.
nn.Parameter(...)Wraps a tensor and tells PyTorch "this is a learnable dial — track its gradient, and include it when someone asks for model.parameters()."
requires_grad=TrueThe actual switch that turns on gradient tracking for this tensor — nn.Parameter sets this for you by default, but it's the mechanism underneath.
torch.randn(1) / torch.rand(1)Random starting guesses — randn draws from a standard normal distribution (can be negative), rand draws uniformly from [0, 1). Neither knows anything about the true weight/bias.
forward(self, x)The only method you're required to write. Defines what happens when you call model(X) — here, literally the linear regression formula, with the model's own (currently wrong) numbers.

3. Loss function and optimizer

loss_fn = nn.L1Loss()                                    # mean absolute error: mean(|y_pred - y_true|)
optimizer = torch.optim.SGD(params=model_0.parameters(), lr=0.01)

nn.L1Loss() is Mean Absolute Error — average the absolute difference between every prediction and its true answer. It's what produced the sharp V shape back in Part 2, instead of a smooth bowl: the penalty for being wrong grows in a straight line, not a curve, and its gradient's size never shrinks as you get close to the right answer — only its sign flips. That single fact is the entire explanation for the oscillation you saw in the contour plot, and it's the entire explanation for the "frozen loss" puzzle below.

4. The training loop, for real

torch.manual_seed(42)
epochs = 100

for epoch in range(epochs):
    model_0.train()
    y_pred = model_0(X_train)              # 1. forward pass
    loss = loss_fn(y_pred, y_train)         # 2. calculate loss
    optimizer.zero_grad()                   # 3. zero grad
    loss.backward()                         # 4. backward pass
    optimizer.step()                        # 5. gradient descent step

    model_0.eval()
    with torch.inference_mode():
        test_pred = model_0(X_test)
        test_loss = loss_fn(test_pred, y_test)

First, what "running a cell again" actually means

A Jupyter notebook has one Python process running behind the scenes, called the kernel. Every cell you run executes inside that same process, sharing the same variables. Running a cell isn't like restarting a program — Python has no idea "this code already ran before." It just sees whatever objects currently exist and keeps going.

Your notebook creates model_0 once, in an earlier cell, with random starting values (weights=0.3367, bias=0.3904). The training-loop cell below it does not recreate model_0 — it just trains whatever model_0 currently is. So the first time you run that cell, it trains the fresh random model for 100 epochs. Run the exact same cell a second time, and Python trains that already-partially-trained model for another 100 epochs — the local loop counter resets to 0 and prints "epoch 0" again, but the model's actual weights and bias just keep moving from wherever the first run left them. Nothing about "epoch 0" in the print statement means "the model is back to its original random values" — it only means "this is iteration 1 of this particular execution of the cell."

i
Does torch.manual_seed(42) at the top of the cell reset anything?

No — and this is a common point of confusion. manual_seed only controls future random number generation (the next torch.randn(...) or torch.rand(...) call). Your training loop never draws new random numbers — it only reads and updates the weights/bias tensors that already exist on model_0. Re-running torch.manual_seed(42) has literally zero effect on a model that already exists; it's a leftover from copying the line into this cell, not a reset switch.

What happens if you run it a third time?

Your model is currently sitting at real epoch 190 (weights=0.6831, bias=0.3069) — very close to the true weights=0.7, bias=0.3, but not there yet. If you run the training-loop cell again without restarting the kernel, the next 100 epochs push it past real epoch 199 — and from there, something specific happens: because nn.L1Loss's gradient never shrinks as the model gets closer to the answer (only its sign flips), fixed-size steps always overshoot the exact minimum once they're close enough, landing in a stable back-and-forth between two fixed values instead of settling on one. I confirmed this by running the simulation much further — from real epoch ~199 onward it locks into alternating between exactly these two states, forever:

The practical takeaway for working in notebooks generally: re-running a training cell continues training instead of restarting it, because the model object persists in the kernel for as long as that kernel is alive. If you want a truly fresh run, re-run the cell that creates model_0 first (or restart the kernel via Jupyter's "Restart Kernel" command), not just the loop cell.
